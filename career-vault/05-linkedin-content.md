### 2. Post one: what I learned shipping the Class 6 screen

"Shipped a tutor-booking summary screen for a BD edtech product this week.
Parent-facing, bKash/Nagad first, mobile only.

The decision that cost the most time wasn't the layout — it was rejecting
the brief's own success metric. It measured tutor signups, but the parent
is the one holding the phone and the payment method on this screen. Once
I mapped that out, the whole information hierarchy had to change: price
and a verified badge moved above the tutor's photo, because on a
throttled 3G connection the photo is the heaviest thing on the page and
was blocking the two things a worried parent actually needs to see first.

The thing I keep relearning: the brief's stated metric and the brief's
actual user are not always the same person, and nobody flags that for
you. You have to go looking for the mismatch yourself.

If you've caught a similar metric-vs-user mismatch in a brief, I'd like to
compare notes on how you argued for the fix."

### 3. Post two: something I believe that most designers around me do not

"A 'verified' badge with no label behind it is worse than no badge at all.

Most badges I see on booking or marketplace screens in this market are a
checkmark in a circle, no text, no explanation. Designers add them because
trust signals are expected, and a checkmark is the fastest way to satisfy
that expectation visually.

But a bare checkmark makes an implicit claim — something was verified —
without saying what, by whom, or when. A parent specifically worried about
an unqualified tutor gets a decoration that looks like reassurance and
carries zero actual information. It would look identical whether or not
any document was ever checked.

If a badge can't survive the question 'verified by whom, checking what,'
it isn't a trust signal. It's a shape that resembles one."
